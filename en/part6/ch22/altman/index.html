    How Sam Altman sees it ★¶

    Ch. 22 · 22.1 · DeeperAbout 8 min read2026-11-09

    How to read this page

    This page shares views and predictions from posts OpenAI's CEO wrote on his own blog. He runs the company that makes ChatGPT, so he has a direct stake in this technology going well. These are claims, not facts, and we kept quotes to a minimum. We couldn't check video interviews from our work environment, so we didn't use them.

    What you'll learn on this page

    • Altman says AI gets smarter in a steady way as you spend more, and the same AI keeps getting much cheaper
    • He makes guesses, year by year, from 2025 into the 2030s. These are predictions.
    • On safety, his plan is: first make AI reliably do what people truly want. Then spread very smart AI widely.

    Three Observations: three claims about the economics¶

    Altman calls these three points observations. They're about money and AI.

    1. The more you spend on a model, the smarter it gets, but with less gain for each extra dollar. (In math terms, its smarts grow with the log of what you spend.) The spending goes on computers for training, data, and computers for answering. He says spending more seems to bring steady, predictable gains, and that the scaling laws predicting this are accurate over many orders of magnitude. (This points the same way as Kaplan and others, 2020, from Chapters 9 and 20.)
    2. The cost of using a given level of AI falls about 10 times every 12 months. His example: from GPT-4 in early 2023 to GPT-4o in mid-2024, the price per token fell about 150 times.
    3. As AI gets smarter step by step, its value to people grows faster and faster. So he sees no reason for the fast-growing spending on AI to stop soon.

    He also says agents (Chapter 14) will start to feel like coworkers who live in the computer. Picture one that writes software, he says. He lists its limits himself. It won't have the biggest new ideas. It will need a lot of watching and direction. It will be great at some things but surprisingly bad at others. Picture it as "a real-but-relatively-junior virtual coworker," he says, and then imagine 1,000 of them, or a million.

    The Gentle Singularity: year-by-year predictions¶

    The June 2025 post opens: "We are past the event horizon; the takeoff has started." Here are its main claims:

    Claim Type
    We've recently built systems smarter than people in many ways His assessment
    In some big sense, ChatGPT is already more powerful than any human who has ever lived His assessment (figurative)
    2025 brought agents that do real thinking work His description
    2026 will likely bring systems that can figure out new insights Prediction
    2027 may bring robots that do tasks in the real world Prediction
    In the 2030s, intelligence and energy will become wildly abundant Prediction
    Scientists say they're two or three times more productive thanks to AI What he reports (source not checked)
    An average ChatGPT query uses about 0.34 watt-hours His figure (not verified)

    The post keeps saying the change will be gentle. It won't all come at once. In the 2030s, people will still love their families, make things, and play. He admits there will be very hard parts, like whole kinds of jobs going away. But he says the world will get richer so fast that new policies become possible.

    His path on safety¶

    In the same post, he admits there are big problems, and lays out a plan:

    1. Solve the alignment problem. By his definition, that means reliably making sure AI systems learn and act toward what we collectively, really want over the long run. He gives social media feeds as an example of misaligned AI: they're great at figuring out what keeps you scrolling right now, but they override what you want in the long run.
    2. Then make very smart AI cheap and easy for everyone to get, so no one person, company, or country holds too much of it.
    3. Give users lots of freedom within broad limits society decides. He says the sooner the world starts talking about what those limits are, the better.

    He also writes that hundreds of millions of people use ChatGPT every day. So a small new skill can do a lot of good. And a small flaw, times hundreds of millions of people, can do a lot of harm.

    ★ Going deeper

    • In these posts he also states his company's mission and values (making AGI benefit all of humanity, being "a superintelligence research company"). Those are statements of goals, not descriptions of fact. Keep in mind that they also serve to justify investment and product launches.
    • In Three Observations, he says one reason OpenAI launches products early and often is to give society and the technology time to co-evolve. Some people disagree with this approach. This book doesn't cover that debate.
    • Reflections also includes personal history, like running the company and being unexpectedly fired in late 2023, which he calls a big failure of governance. We left it out because it's not close to this book's topic.

    Try it yourself¶

    A prediction checklist. From the year-by-year table above, pick the 2026 and 2027 predictions and put them on your calendar.

    Prediction When to check How to check
    2026: systems that figure out new insights End of 2026 Look for cases accepted as "new insights"
    2027: robots doing real-world tasks End of 2027 Look for what tasks they do, and how well

    By the time you read this (November 2026 or later), you may already be able to check some of it. See for yourself how the predictions held up.

    Use it today¶

    1. Remember predictions along with their dates. Then you can check later whether they came true.
    2. Read a CEO's writing with their interests in mind. Upbeat forecasts may line up with the company's interests.
    3. Separate "observations" from "predictions." Both are mixed in the same posts.
    4. Notice where he admits limits too. Like the agent that's "surprisingly bad at others."
    5. Read other people alongside him. On the next pages, Hinton and Amodei stress different things.

    Common mix-ups¶

    • "The predictions in these posts will happen soon." They're predictions.
    • "Altman denies the risks." He writes that safety problems must be solved. His emphasis is different.
    • "Scaling laws are proven laws of nature." As Chapter 20 showed, there's debate.
