    Comparing views: where they differ ★¶

    Ch. 22 · 22.1 · DeeperAbout 9 min read2026-11-09

    How to read this page

    This page compares only what we read on the last five pages. Things we didn't read, like videos and newer talks, aren't included. We don't judge who is right. In the tables, "Not found" means "not in what we read." It doesn't mean the person disagrees.

    What you'll learn on this page

    • Experts in the same field differ on speed, which risks matter most, fixes, and their own interests.
    • Even so, four of them signed the same one-sentence statement about the risk of extinction from AI.
    • You'll learn eight questions for reading what any expert says.

    Same statement, different stories¶

    In 2023, the Center for AI Safety (CAIS) put out a one-sentence statement. Its point, in our words: lowering the risk of human extinction from AI should be a world priority, next to other society-wide risks like pandemics and nuclear war.

    Four people from this chapter are on its signer list: Geoffrey Hinton (listed as emeritus professor, University of Toronto), Demis Hassabis (CEO, Google DeepMind), Sam Altman (CEO, OpenAI), and Dario Amodei (CEO, Anthropic). Yoshua Bengio and Ilya Sutskever are on it too. We didn't find Karpathy's name in the part of the list we could see.

    It's one sentence. Signing it doesn't mean they agree on the details. As you'll see below, they don't.

    Where they split¶

    1. Where the weight of risk falls

    • Hinton talks about risks already happening and possible loss of control later. He admits he has no simple recipe.
    • Altman admits safety problems, but his posts lean toward abundance and gentle change. His path is to solve alignment, then spread AI widely.
    • Amodei sees both a big upside and big risks. He puts weight on one technical fix: interpretability.
    • Hassabis names misuse by bad actors and control of more independent AI. He calls the technology itself fairly neutral.

    2. Who should act, and how

    • Hinton: governments force big companies to put more into safety research.
    • Altman: society sets broad limits, and no one person, company, or country holds too much.
    • Amodei: researchers, companies, and governments all push interpretability. He suggests light-touch rules and asks other companies to do more.
    • Hassabis: society decides together, with builders talking to governments, schools, businesses, and the public.

    3. How sure they are about speed

    • Altman and Amodei name 2026 and 2027. (Altman: new insights and robots. Amodei: a "country of geniuses.")
    • Karpathy's prediction is for 2055 (written in 2022). Hassabis says only "the foreseeable future." Hinton says "the next few years," but about the need to find answers, not about what AI will do.
    • They're predicting different things (what AI can do, how society changes, how big models get). So you can't line them up directly.

    4. Does AI "understand"?

    • Hinton says large language models really do understand, and the linguists who say they don't are wrong. He also says it's still hotly debated.
    • We didn't find a clear position on this from the other four in what we read.

    Who's speaking?¶

    Person Kind of source Keep in mind
    Hinton Prize speech and interview In what we read, he doesn't speak for a company
    Altman A CEO's blog He has a direct stake in AI going well
    Amodei A CEO's essays He runs Anthropic. The AI that helped write this book was made by Anthropic
    Hassabis Prize interviews A CEO speaking; he defends his company's research setup
    Karpathy A personal blog No title checked by us (we didn't watch his videos)

    Having a stake doesn't make someone wrong. It means you read the same words a little differently, depending on who says them.

    Eight questions for reading any expert¶

    1. Who said it? What's their job, and who do they work for?
    2. When? Views can change.
    3. Where? A speech, a blog, an interview, and a paper are different things.
    4. Is it a fact, a view, or a prediction? Mark each one.
    5. What's the evidence? A study, or one person's judgment?
    6. Who gains? Does anyone benefit if you believe it?
    7. Did they admit limits? Look for where they say "we don't know."
    8. What do others say? Don't decide from one side.

    ★ Going deeper

    • This comparison rests on one or two sources per person. It doesn't stand for everything each of them has said.
    • Interviews and talks can be edited or quoted out of context. Read the full text when you can.
    • Many voices aren't here. Many researchers weigh AI's risks and upside differently from these five. We didn't check their work, so we don't summarize it.
    • The table cells are our short summaries. Go back to each person's page for the details and sources.

    Try it yourself¶

    Add a sixth column.

    1. Pick one person this book didn't cover, a researcher or a business leader.
    2. Find something they wrote or said in full. Not a news summary; the original.
    3. Fill in the rows: what you read, role, upside, risks, fix, timing. Write "Not found" where you don't know.
    4. Run the eight questions on it.

    Use it today¶

    1. When you see a bold claim, make a quick table. Who, when, what, and based on what. That alone cuts through hype.
    2. Signing the same statement isn't the same as agreeing. Separate a one-line "yes" from detailed views.
    3. Write down dated predictions and check them later. 2026 and 2027 are passing now.
    4. Respect "we don't know." Sometimes a person who admits doubt is more trustworthy than one who sounds sure.
    5. Look for people who disagree. This chapter shows only a few voices.

    Common mix-ups¶

    • "AI experts all say the same thing." They differ on speed, which risks matter, and fixes.
    • "A famous person said it, so it's true." Most of it is views and predictions.
    • "All warnings about AI are hype" or "all of them are true." Check the evidence before you pick a side.
