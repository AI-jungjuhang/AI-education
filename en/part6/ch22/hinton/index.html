    How Geoffrey Hinton sees it ★¶

    Ch. 22 · 22.1 · DeeperAbout 8 min read2026-11-09

    How to read this page

    This page shares views from a short speech and a phone interview by Geoffrey Hinton, who won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics. They're his opinions and worries, not settled facts. We kept quotes to a minimum and put the rest in our own words. Other experts disagree with parts of what he says.

    What you'll learn on this page

    • Hinton sees a big upside: AI assistants that raise how much people get done in almost every job.
    • He lists risks for today and the near future, and a bigger, long-term worry about staying in control of AI smarter than us.
    • He asks governments to act, and to make big companies spend more on safety research.

    Which sources?¶

    Hinton helped build the neural network methods behind today's AI (Chapters 9 and 19). In 2024 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for that work. We read these:

    Source Date Note
    Nobel banquet speech 2024-12-10 A short speech; the whole text is on the Nobel site
    Phone interview with the Nobel site October 2024 Recorded just after he heard about the prize. Adam Smith of the Nobel site asked the questions
    Statement on AI Risk (Center for AI Safety) Around May 30, 2023 (estimated) A one-sentence statement. He is listed as a signer

    What he said in the speech¶

    He spoke about three things.

    1. The good side. This new kind of AI, he said, is good at copying human intuition more than human step-by-step reasoning. It will let us build very smart, knowing assistants that make almost every industry more productive. If the gains are shared fairly, he said, it will be a wonderful step forward for everyone.

    2. Risks now and soon. He listed these:

    Risk When, in his words
    Echo chambers that split people, by showing content that makes them angry Already happening
    Governments that don't allow freedom, using AI to watch huge numbers of people Already happening
    Criminals using AI for phishing (fake messages that trick you) Already happening
    Making terrible new viruses May happen soon
    Deadly weapons that decide on their own who to hurt or kill May happen soon

    He said all of these need urgent, strong attention from governments and international groups.

    3. A long-term risk. He sees a threat to humanity's survival once we build digital beings smarter than we are. He said we have no idea whether we can stay in control. He also said there's now evidence that if such beings are made by companies chasing short-term profit, our safety won't come first. So we urgently need research on how to keep them from wanting to take control. These things, he said, are no longer science fiction.

    What he said in the phone interview¶

    • It's not like climate change. With climate change, he said, everyone knows what to do: stop burning carbon. The hard part is political will. With AI, we have much less idea what will happen or what to do. He said he wishes he had a simple recipe, but he doesn't.
    • A fork in the road. On the threat of AI getting out of control, he said we're at a turning point in history. In the next few years we need to work out whether there's a way to handle it.
    • What governments can do. He said governments could force big companies to spend much more on safety research. Then a company like OpenAI couldn't push safety to the back burner.
    • A past example. He was asked about the Asilomar meetings, where biologists met to agree on how to handle a risky new technology. He said it was a good model. But AI has far more practical uses, so it will be much harder.
    • Do language models understand? He said they really do understand what they're saying. He thinks the school of language study that follows Chomsky, which says they don't, is wrong. He added that there's still a lot of debate, mostly among linguists.

    The statement he signed¶

    In 2023, the Center for AI Safety put out a one-sentence statement. Its point: lowering the risk of human extinction from AI should be a world priority, next to other society-wide risks like pandemics and nuclear war. Hinton is on the list of signers. So are Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Demis Hassabis, whose views are on the other pages of this chapter. Signing one sentence doesn't tell you how each person thinks we should act.

    What kind of claim is each one?¶

    Type Example from Hinton
    What he describes as happening now Echo chambers, mass surveillance, phishing
    What he says may happen New viruses, weapons that pick targets
    His worry Losing control of AI smarter than us
    What he admits is unknown Whether we can stay in control; what to do
    His judgment Language models really understand; companies put profit first
    His proposal Government action; more company money for safety research

    ★ Going deeper

    • Hinton names a company (OpenAI) in the interview. That's his view of how companies act, not a finding we checked.
    • The speech gives no evidence for "we now have evidence." We don't know what evidence he meant, so we don't guess.
    • Whether language models "understand" depends partly on what you mean by the word. That's why scientists and linguists still argue about it.
    • There's also a longer interview from December 2024 on the Nobel site, with Swedish TV. We didn't use it.

    Try it yourself¶

    Sort his claims (10 minutes). Copy the five risks from the speech table. Next to each, write:

    1. Does it touch your life or work now? (Yes, maybe, no)
    2. Is there something you can do about it yourself? For phishing, for example, check links before you click.
    3. Is it something only governments or companies can handle?

    You'll likely find that some of his "now" risks are ones you can shrink today, and others need society to act.

    Use it today¶

    1. Watch for phishing that sounds smooth. AI makes fake messages read well. Check the sender and the link (Chapter 3).
    2. Notice content that makes you angry. Ask whether a feed is showing it to keep you scrolling.
    3. Separate "happening now" from "may happen." Hinton marks this himself. Keep the line clear when you repeat it.
    4. Notice what he admits he doesn't know. He says he has no simple recipe.
    5. Read the other views too. The next pages show Amodei and Hassabis, who stress different things.

    Common mix-ups¶

    • "Hinton says AI is bad." He also expects big gains, if they're shared fairly.
    • "Hinton says we'll lose control." He says we have no idea whether we can stay in control. That's uncertainty, not a prediction of loss.
    • "Everyone who signed the statement agrees on what to do." It's one sentence about priority. How to act is left open.
