# Copyright and plagiarism

<p class="ep-meta"><span>Ch. 3 · 3.2</span><span>About 5 min read</span><span>2026-11-09</span></p>

!!! abstract "What you'll learn on this page"
    - Before you use text or images a chatbot made, think about ==who owns it, and whether it looks like someone else's work==.
    - There are things to watch both when you paste in other people's work and when you put out the result.
    - Schools and workplaces may have their own rules about using AI.

!!! warning "Please note"
    This page is general information, not legal advice. Laws and government guidance change, and they differ from country to country. For anything important, check with an expert or the right office.

## Copying a friend's homework

You shouldn't copy a friend's homework word for word. Handing it in as your own is even worse. And if your teacher says, "You can use other work for ideas, but list your sources," you follow that rule.

Writing from a chatbot is similar. A chatbot learned from huge amounts of text, so what it writes may end up looking like something already out there. And where you're allowed to use the result depends on laws and rules.

!!! tip "In 10-year-old words"
    Before you copy a chatbot's writing into your homework, check twice. Does it look like someone else's work? Are you allowed to use it there?

## Three situations

**1. When you paste in someone else's work**

- Pasting in a whole book, article, or someone else's report may break the service's rules or copyright.
- Quote only the part you need. When you can, ask it to explain in its own words instead.

**2. When you use what the chatbot made**

- Check that the result doesn't look too much like an existing work. Be extra careful if it resembles famous song lyrics, lines from a novel, or a well-known character. (Looking alike isn't always illegal. Courts weigh many things.)
- If you'll use the text or images to make money, read the service's terms about how you may use outputs.

**3. When you put your name on it**

- School assignments, contests, and research papers often have rules about whether AI is allowed and how to say you used it. Check the rules first.
- Hiding AI use can get you in trouble. And even when the rules allow it, checking the facts is still your job (remember hallucination).

## What the US Copyright Office says

In January 2025, the US Copyright Office released Part 2 of its report on copyright and AI. It covers whether things made with AI can be protected by copyright. Here are the points most useful to everyday users.

- AI output can be protected only where a **human author** decided enough of the creative parts.
- That can include human work you can see in the output, or creative choices a human made in arranging or changing it. Just typing a prompt is not enough.
- Using AI to help you create, or adding AI-made parts into a larger work you wrote, does not on its own stop your work from being protected.

This is the view of one US office. Other countries may see it differently.

!!! note "★ Going deeper"
    - Whether it's legal for AI to learn from copyrighted work (for example, under US "fair use") is still being argued in many countries. Court rulings and policies keep changing. The US Copyright Office released a pre-publication version of Part 3 of its report, on training AI, in May 2025.
    - For you as a user, one thing is clear for now. Until the legal questions settle, the person who uses the output carries much of the job of checking it and answering for it.

## Try it yourself

1. Ask a chatbot for 5 short ad slogans.
2. Pick one you like. Search for it with quotation marks around the whole slogan, like "your slogan here."
3. See whether a similar slogan is already in use.
4. Find the part of your chatbot's terms of service about rights to outputs, and read it.

## Use it today

1. **Search a sentence before you use it.** It's the easiest way to see if a similar line or ad already exists.
2. **Ask for an explanation, not a copy.** "Sum up the main idea of this article in your own words" is safer than "Write it just like this article."
3. **Check your school's or workplace's AI rules first.** Learn what's allowed and how to note that you used AI.
4. **Make a habit of saying when you used AI.** If needed, add a line like "AI was used to help draft this."
5. **For business use, read the terms.** Each service has its own rules about using outputs.

## Common mix-ups

- "AI made it, so nobody owns it." It depends on the country and the situation. A key question is how much creative work a human did.
- "I only asked for it, so I'm not responsible." The person who puts out the result needs to check it.
- "If I change a few words, it's not plagiarism." It depends on how close it is and on the rules. Copying something and tweaking it can still be a problem.
