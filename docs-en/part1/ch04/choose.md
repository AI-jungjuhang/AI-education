# Pick the AI that fits your work

<p class="ep-meta"><span>Ch. 4 · 4.1</span><span>About 5 min read</span><span>2026-11-09</span></p>

!!! abstract "What you'll learn on this page"
    - When choosing a tool, don't start with the brand name. Start with ==the job you want done==.
    - Narrow down your choices with four questions: what kind of job, how exact, how sensitive, and how often.
    - Once you pick, test it on your real work, and check again now and then.

## Picking shoes

You wear different shoes for a walk, a run, or a hike. The best shoes aren't the most expensive ones. They're the ones that fit what you're about to do.

AI is the same. What you're going to do matters more than which service is famous.

!!! tip "In 10-year-old words"
    Before asking "Which one is best?", ask "What am I going to do with it?"

## Four questions

**1. What kind of job is it?** Writing, research, getting organized, pictures, or code? (See the table of kinds on the earlier page.)

**2. How exact does it need to be?** Ideas can be a bit off. Numbers, law, and medicine must be checked. The more accuracy matters, the more you need search, sources, and a human check.

**3. How sensitive is it?** If personal details or work secrets are involved, data settings and rules decide your choice. (See the privacy page in 3.2.)

**4. How often will you do it?** For a one-off, almost anything works. For a daily job, usage limits, reliability, and a way to save templates matter.

## Where to start, by job

| Job | Look at first |
|---|---|
| Polishing writing, first drafts | A text chatbot. Paste in your own material |
| Recent news, fact checks | A tool with search, and open the sources |
| Reading long documents | A feature for uploading long files, and its limits |
| Numbers and tables | Check the math separately with a tool |
| Pictures | An image tool, and read its terms on copyright |
| Work tasks | The tools your workplace has approved, and data settings |

## How to test

1. **Give the same job to two tools.** Use the job you do most often, so the test means something.
2. **Compare with a checklist.** Note accuracy, tone, sources, speed, and anything annoying.
3. **Look for mistakes on purpose.** Is anything wrong? When it's wrong, does it admit it?
4. **Once you decide, check again in about a month.** (That's our suggestion.) Services change fast.

```text
[Test job]
The job I do most often: ___
The result I expect: ___
What to check: Are the facts right? Is the tone right? How long did it take?
```

!!! note "★ Going deeper"
    - Anthropic's guide to choosing a model says to **set your needs first, then confirm with real tests**. It's written for developers, but it works for everyone. The needs are capabilities, speed, and cost. The tests should use examples that look like your real work.
    - Public score charts (benchmarks) are useful to glance at, but whether they match your work is another question. Part VI covers the limits of benchmarks.

## Use it today

1. **Decide the job first, then the tool.** If you do it the other way around, you end up bending your work to fit the tool.
2. **For jobs that must be exact, plan your checking too.** Use the checking methods from Chapter 3.
3. **For work, start with approved tools.** Privacy and secrets come first when you choose.
4. **Test with your real work.** Someone else's examples may not look like your job.
5. **Check in once a month.** A better tool may appear, or a feature you use may change.

## Common mix-ups

- "What everyone uses must be the best." What matters is whether it fits your work.
- "Once I pick, I'm set for good." Services change, and so does your work. Check now and then.
- "Number one on the score chart means number one for my job." Score charts measure different problems from yours.
