## Weather and earthquakes

A weather forecast tells you whether to bring an umbrella today. Earthquake preparation is for something rare but serious. Both are about risk, but they need **different kinds of thinking**.

AI risk feels less overwhelming split this way. **Small risks** in your daily work can be handled right away. **Big risks**, rare but serious, are something society works on together.

!!! tip "In 10-year-old words"
    Think of AI risks as umbrella weather (today's risks in your work) and earthquake planning (rare but big risks).

## Five questions for any risk

For each job or situation, ask these. (They're our own summary.)

1. **Who gets hurt?** Just you, or customers and other people too?
2. **How bad is it?** A hassle, or does it touch money, health, law, or safety?
3. **Can it be undone?** Can you cancel or recover?
4. **How likely is it?** Is this an area where it often gets things wrong?
5. **Who's responsible?** Is there someone who checks before results go out?

This is a bigger version of the checklist in Chapter 8.

## What expert groups are doing

The **International AI Safety Report** (January 2025) is one example. According to its summary, it pulls together current evidence on what advanced AI can do, its risks, and its safety. It was requested by the countries at the AI Safety Summit in Bletchley, UK. **Thirty countries, the UN, the OECD, and the EU** each named a representative to its expert panel, and **100 AI experts** contributed. The independent experts, led by the report's chair, had full say over what it contains. (We read only the summary, not the report's detailed list of risks.)

The US **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** is a voluntary framework to help organizations make AI more trustworthy. It came out on January 26, 2023, and a profile on risks specific to generative AI (NIST-AI-600-1) followed on July 26, 2024 (we checked these in Chapter 8).

## Experts disagree on the big risks

On big or long-term risks from AI, expert opinions differ widely. Some see them as very serious. Others think they're overblown. Chapter 22 looks at what some of the people who built this field have said publicly, noting **who said it, when, and where**. For now, remember:

- **Be wary of anything that shows only one side.** "We're doomed soon" and "There's nothing to worry about" both need evidence.
- **A prediction is a prediction.** Be extra careful with confident claims tied to a specific year.
- **Tell evidence apart from opinion.** A research finding and a researcher's opinion are different things.

!!! note "★ Going deeper"
    - As Chapter 19 showed, you can't confirm a model's **inner goals or calculations** from its outward behavior alone. So safety research uses testing (Chapter 21) and looking inside (Chapter 19) together.
    - The safety framework in Chapter 14, "keep people in control while giving freedom," can be read as saying that **safety in the technology and safety in how it's used** are both needed. (That's our reading.)
    - There's also disagreement about how, and how fast, to reduce risks (laws, voluntary rules, slowing development, and more). This book doesn't cover those policy debates.

## Use it today

1. **Ask the five questions for each job.** Risks differ from job to job.
2. **Do things that can't be undone yourself.** Deleting, paying, sending, and signing.
3. **Check the source of risk news.** Who said it, and based on what?
4. **Don't be swayed by hype in either direction.** Separate evidence from opinion.
5. **Shrink the small risks in your own work first.** Today's habits affect you more than the big-risk debate does.

## Common mix-ups

- "AI risk is a far-future movie plot." Small, real risks show up in daily work right now.
- "All the talk about AI risk is hype." Experts take it seriously enough to write reports. But their views differ.
- "To avoid risk, don't use AI." There's a middle path: know the risks and reduce them as you use it.
