    This page shares **views and predictions from two essays** by Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic. Anthropic makes Claude, the AI this book uses most.     - Amodei says most people ==underrate both how good AI could be and how bad its risks could be==.
    - He thinks we can't stop AI from moving forward, but we can steer how it happens.
    - He calls it a race: we need to ==understand what's inside AI before it gets too powerful==.

## *Machines of Loving Grace*: the hopeful side

The essay starts by clearing up a mix-up. He talks about AI risks a lot, so people sometimes think he's a "doomer" who expects AI to be mostly bad. He says that's not true at all. He focuses on risks because, in his view, **they're the only thing standing between us and a good future**. He thinks most people underrate **how big the upside** could be, and also **how bad the risks** could be.

He says no one can know the future, so all of this is **guesses**. He gives lots of detail anyway, because he thinks a clear picture helps people discuss it better than a vague one.

He also explains why he and Anthropic usually talk more about risks than benefits:

1. **That's where action counts.** Much of AI's progress, and many of its benefits, seem bound to happen because of market forces. The risks aren't fixed, so what we do can change them a lot.
2. **To not sound like an ad.** An AI company that only praises AI can look like it's hiding the downsides.
3. **To avoid acting like a prophet.** He says it's dangerous to think one company shapes the world alone, or to treat tech goals almost like religion.
4. **To avoid a sci-fi feel.** Talk of uploaded minds and space travel makes people take the claims less seriously.

**Key predictions in the essay:**

- **Powerful AI could come as early as 2026**, he wrote, though it could take much longer. (He dislikes the term AGI and says "powerful AI" instead.)
- He pictures it as smarter than a Nobel Prize winner in most fields, running in millions of copies. He sums this up as **"a country of geniuses in a datacenter."**
- In biology and medicine, he guesses AI could pack **50 to 100 years of progress into 5 to 10 years**. He calls this the "compressed 21st century."

He writes that he'll surely get plenty wrong. He also covers other areas (the mind, poverty, peace, and work), but we read only the parts above.

## *The Urgency of Interpretability*: the worried side

**The bus.** After about ten years in AI, he says his biggest lesson is that the technology moves forward with forces too strong to stop. But we can change **what gets built first, which uses we choose, and how it reaches people**. His short version: **"We can't stop the bus, but we can steer it."**

**Why look inside?** (This is the topic of Chapter 19.)

- Normal software does what people write. Today's AI isn't written line by line, so we can't say exactly **why it picks certain words, or why it's sometimes wrong**. He shares his cofounder Chris Olah's view that these systems are **grown more than built**, like a plant.
- Many worries about AI, like lying or seeking power, come from this **not being able to see inside**.
- He says there's **no solid evidence yet** of lying or power-seeking in real-world use. But such behavior could appear on its own during training. Just asking a model is like asking someone "Are you a terrorist?" You learn something, but it's clearly not reliable.

**His predictions and goals:**

| Claim | Type |
|---|---|
| Recent research shows a real path to an **"MRI for AI"** | His assessment |
| On the current path, interpretability could get there **within 5 to 10 years** | **Prediction** (he'd "bet strongly") |
| A "country of geniuses in a datacenter" could come **as soon as 2026 or 2027** | **Prediction** |
| So it's a **race** between understanding AI and AI getting smarter | His framing |
| Anthropic aims to **reliably find most model problems by 2027** | **His company's goal** |

He says every step forward helps, even before the goal is reached. He asks for three things:

1. **Researchers** at companies, universities, and nonprofits should work on it more. He names Google DeepMind and OpenAI as companies that should put more into it.
2. **Governments** should use **light-touch rules**. It's too early to require "AI MRI" checks, he says, since no one knows yet what a law should ask for. But companies could be required to **share their safety practices**, including how they test models before release.
3. Governments can use **limits on exporting chips** to buy more time.

## Use it today

1. **Don't judge a model only by what it says about itself.** Check what it does (Chapters 3 and 21).
2. **Steer, don't just accept.** In your own work, you choose which jobs to give AI and how to check them.
3. **Keep a date next to each prediction.** "2026 or 2027" is checkable soon.
4. **Read hope and worry together.** He gives both. Notice when a writer gives only one.
5. **Remember who's writing.** A CEO writing about his own field has interests, even when he's careful.

## Common mix-ups

- "Amodei is a doomer." He says the opposite. He focuses on risks because he sees a good future behind them.
- "We already have an MRI for AI." It's a goal and a prediction, not something done.
- "He says AI is already lying to us." He says there's no solid real-world evidence yet, but it could happen.
