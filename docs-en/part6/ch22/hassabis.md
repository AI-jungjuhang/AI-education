# How Demis Hassabis sees it ★

<p class="ep-meta"><span>Ch. 22 · 22.1 · Deeper</span><span>About 8 min read</span><span>2026-11-09</span></p>

!!! warning "How to read this page"
    This page shares **views from two interviews** with Demis Hassabis, the CEO of Google DeepMind. He shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, an AI that predicts the shapes of proteins. His company builds AI, so he has **a direct stake in it**. These are his views, not facts. We kept quotes to a minimum and put the rest in our own words.

!!! abstract "What you'll learn on this page"
    - Hassabis sees AI as ==a tool that helps scientists do far more==, while the questions still come from people.
    - He names two worries: ==bad actors misusing AI==, and keeping control of AI as it gets more powerful and acts more on its own.
    - He says society should decide together what AI should and shouldn't be used for.

## AI as a tool for science (phone interview)

**Why he works on AI.** He says he's worked on AI his whole life because he loves science and finding things out. He's always thought that AI, **built the right way**, could be the ultimate tool to help scientists explore the universe. He hopes AlphaFold is a first example.

**Scientists still matter.** Asked where this leaves the individual scientist, he said that **at least for the foreseeable future**, AI lets each scientist do much more. These systems are **tools**. They're very good at analyzing data and finding patterns in it. But they can't figure out **the right question to ask**, or the right guess to test. That has to come from the human scientist. He thinks top scientists with these tools could do amazing things, maybe even **in smaller teams**. The tools do a lot of the legwork.

**Where research happens.** He says he designed Google DeepMind to be a perfect place for cutting-edge research. It brings together experts in AI, engineering, physics, biology, and even philosophy. He was inspired by stories of the golden years of places like Bell Labs. Asked whether it matters that the work happens at a company, not a university, he said he doesn't think so. Great science can happen anywhere if it's done the right way. New fields need lots of computers, which cost a lot, so why not use private money?

## Hopes and worries (Nobel Week interview)

**The possibilities.** He says AI's possibilities are almost unlimited. Human intelligence built modern civilization, so we shouldn't be surprised that intelligence is a powerful force. AI research, he says, tries to understand intelligence by building it. If it's built in a general enough way, it could be used on almost any problem, much like the human mind.

**Making it good for everyone.** He says this is hard, and partly outside the technology itself. It's a question about countries and power. He expects big gains in productivity, the economy, and science. Society should **decide together what to use AI for and what not to use it for**, and how to share the benefits. He says people who build AI should talk widely with governments, universities, businesses, and the public.

**His two worries:**

1. **Bad actors.** AI is a general-purpose technology. It can help with medicine and climate, but bad actors could also **turn it to harmful uses**. He calls the technology itself fairly neutral. What matters is how people choose to use it.
2. **Control.** As systems get more powerful, **act more on their own, and behave more like agents**, can we understand them and **keep control**? Can we make sure they have the right goals and values, and that their guardrails are strong enough?

**His hopes.** He hopes AI helps solve some of our biggest problems. He names cures for terrible diseases, perhaps with tools like AlphaFold. He also names climate change, maybe through new materials, fusion, or better batteries.

If AI did the first or the last row, try taking those back. Hassabis's view is that those rows belong to people.

## Use it today

1. **Pick the question yourself.** Let AI do the legwork, not the deciding.
2. **Treat AI as a tool that can be misused.** Watch for scams and fakes made with it (Chapter 3).
3. **Give agents limits.** The more an AI acts on its own, the more you need approvals and limits (Chapter 14).
4. **Notice limits like "for the foreseeable future."** They change what a claim means.
5. **Read across the chapter.** Each person stresses different things. The next page lines them up.

## Common mix-ups

- "Hassabis says AI will replace scientists." He says the opposite, at least for the foreseeable future.
- "He isn't worried about AI." He names two worries: misuse and keeping control.
- "AI decides what's worth studying." In his view, people choose the questions.
